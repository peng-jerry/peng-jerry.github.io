Another important project I worked on was the Launch-a-Birdie project. 

Most badminton players don't have consistent access to a training partner skilled enough to feed them high-volume, repetitive shots — the kind of practice needed to actually improve. Through surveys and interviews with players and coaches, I found that ~78% of players lacked reliable access to a quality practice partner, and most trained only 1–2 days a week. I set out with my team to design a low-cost, semester-buildable robotic launcher that could fill that gap.

We broke the system into four subsystems — storage, feeding, aiming, and launching — and worked through structured concept generation, decision matrices, and CAD development for each. I helped drive the selection process, weighing options like servo gimbals vs. rotating bases, star-wheel feeders vs. gravity chutes, and flywheels vs. pneumatic launchers against cost, reliability, and manufacturability. We landed on a rotating base for aiming, a star-wheel indexer for feeding, dual counter-rotating flywheels for launching, and a simple gravity tube for storage.

I primarily worked on the electrical wiring 

This project taught me as much about systems integration and team process as it did about mechanical design. Working in subsystem silos caused avoidable rework — a lesson in defining clear interfaces (geometry, timing, mounting) early and checking in often. On the technical side, I learned that tolerancing and alignment at subsystem interfaces mattered more than raw structural strength — feeding reliability, not the FEA-validated arm, ended up being our biggest limiting factor.
